Ohio has thousands of vacant and poorly managed rental properties sitting alongside a real affordable-housing shortage, with very little connecting the two. OHARP proposes closing that gap with three linked mechanisms, each doing a different job.

The first is accountability: any landlord who lives outside Ohio, or outside the property's home county entirely, has to appoint a local agent who actually lives in-state and is on the hook for code violations and management issues — modeled directly on Cleveland's Residents First program. Owners register annually, and noncompliance carries real consequences: fines, liens, or being barred from renting until the property is brought up to code. The second is the vacancy tax itself. A property sitting empty for six consecutive months without a genuine attempt to rent, sell, or renovate it starts accruing an annual tax on its value, scaled up the longer it stays vacant — 1% in year one, 2% in year two, and so on — with exemptions for anything actively listed or mid-renovation.

The third piece is where the money goes, and it's the part that makes this more than a punitive measure: vacancy-tax revenue funds tax credits for developers who convert vacant properties into affordable units, rental assistance for low-income families, and grants for community land trusts, small-business reuse of long-vacant buildings, and historic preservation. A statewide rental registry and a strengthened housing-court system are the enforcement backbone tying the local-agent requirement and the vacancy tax together — without them, either mechanism is just a rule nobody checks.

Funding starts with the vacancy-tax revenue itself, backstopped by state budget allocations for the registry and enforcement infrastructure in the early years, with federal housing and community-development grants layered on for anything vacancy-tax revenue alone won't cover. The goal isn't to punish landlords for its own sake — it's to make holding a property vacant more expensive than putting it to use.
