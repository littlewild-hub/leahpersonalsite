The word "charity" does real damage, and it's not just a branding complaint. Calling a food pantry, a domestic-violence shelter, or a youth mentoring program "charity" frames it as optional goodwill rather than what it actually is: a piece of infrastructure standing in for a gap government left open. That framing has consequences — when a bridge fails, it's an emergency; when a nonprofit program closes for lack of funding, it's "unfortunate," and the systemic failure underneath it disappears from the conversation entirely.

The piece argues for language that matches the actual function: a "public health infrastructure" rather than a free clinic, a "community safety strategy" rather than a violence-prevention nonprofit. It's a small linguistic move with a real target — treating the sector as a co-builder of public systems rather than a charitable afterthought, and holding government accountable for what it's effectively outsourced without funding properly.
