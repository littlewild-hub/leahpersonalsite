Ohio's labor shortages in construction, healthcare, renewable energy, and advanced manufacturing aren't going to close themselves, and a four-year degree isn't the right answer for a lot of people who could fill them. OSWAP targets four sectors specifically — trades (electricians, plumbers, HVAC), healthcare (CNAs, medical assistants, home health aides), renewable energy (solar and wind installers, efficiency auditors), and advanced manufacturing (machinists, robotics technicians, welders) — chosen for growth potential and labor shortage together, not one or the other.

The incentive structure is built to stack rather than to offer a single flat credit. A business hiring a certified vocational worker gets a base credit of 10% of first-year wages, capped at $5,000 per employee, with an extra 5% for operating in a rural or underserved county. Creating a registered apprenticeship adds a separate $2,000 credit (plus $1,000 more if it's rural), a wage subsidy covering up to 25% of the apprentice's pay for up to two years, and a $1,500 credit for each certified mentor supervising them. Retention is incentivized on its own: keep an apprentice for a year past the program and the business claims another $3,000; keep any certified worker for two years and there's a further $2,000 retention credit, plus upskilling credits covering 10% of further training costs. A business doing all of this in an underserved county can legitimately stack five or six credits on a single hire — that's deliberate, meant to make the paperwork worth doing rather than a token gesture.

Administration runs through a task force under Ohio's Department of Labor, with a single online portal for applications, apprentice tracking, and documentation, and regional "Apprenticeship Hubs" connecting businesses to training providers in underserved areas. Funding draws from three sources: reprioritized state workforce-development dollars, federal grants (DOL's Apprenticeship Building America program, DOE grants for the renewable-energy sectors), and — same funding lever CASSI uses — a share of Ohio's potential recreational-marijuana tax revenue, targeted specifically at rural and underserved-county incentives.
