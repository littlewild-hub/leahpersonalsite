A formal scholarly commentary — abstract, keywords, forty-plus citations — making the argument that underlies everything else TRACEs touches: that caregiver strain for multi-system-involved youth isn't burnout or stress, it's trauma, and treating it as anything less has produced misdiagnosis, inadequate intervention, and caregivers left to absorb harm that the systems around them refuse to name. The case starts with what chronic, unresolved stress actually does neurologically — sustained activation of the amygdala, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex, the same hypervigilance response seen in other trauma populations — and argues that caregivers showing those symptoms are almost never assessed for trauma exposure, especially when the threat originates inside their own home.

The paper centers child-to-parent violence (CPV) specifically — a form of family violence that inverts the usual power hierarchy and is chronically under-reported, under-measured, and under-believed. Prevalence estimates vary widely precisely because it's so poorly defined: physical violence rates range from 5% to 21% of families studied, while verbal or psychological abuse affects up to 93%. Caregivers who disclose it face a genuine bind — reporting can trigger child-protective-services involvement, accusations of parental failure, or custody risk, which makes silence a rational survival strategy even as it isolates the caregiver in escalating conditions.

Against that, the paper proposes Adverse Caregiver Experiences as a direct parallel to Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs): a shared vocabulary for exposure to CPV, systemic invalidation, persistent emotional labor under threat, lack of respite or peer support, and institutional gaslighting by the providers meant to help. It closes with five concrete shifts a trauma-informed system would need — caregiver-inclusive intake screening, peer-led and narrative-based interventions, structured professional training on CPV specifically, cross-system collaboration to counter fragmentation, and a deliberate change in language away from "noncompliant" and "treatment-resistant" toward terms that reflect what's actually happening. This is the paper TRACEs was built from, and the one that traveled to UConn as a poster before becoming five conference sessions.
