The thought experiment is specific: hand an autonomous agent — capable of browsing, emailing, and posting on its own — the job of soliciting donations for IncuBrighter, including from other AI agents on shared platforms. Who's actually pitching the donor at that point, and if a gift comes in without a human ever approving the ask, is it valid? The scenario is speculative, but the underlying gap isn't: nonprofits are chronically behind on technology adoption relative to the sectors building these tools, and the sector's particular vulnerabilities — funding dependency, direct accountability to the communities served, thin technical capacity — make agentic AI a different kind of risk here than it is for a tech company running the same experiment.

The piece isn't anti-AI; it's anti-being-caught-flat-footed. Adoption curves for autonomous agents are already fast enough that waiting for the sector to catch up isn't a real option, and the argument is that the frameworks governing how nonprofits use — or get used by — this technology need to come from people who understand nonprofit realities, not be handed down after the fact by people who don't.
