Ten Ohio counties — Scioto, Vinton, Meigs, Lawrence, Gallia, Jackson, Pike, Ross, Adams, and Monroe, effectively the Appalachian southeast — carry some of the highest rates of poverty, substance use, and unmet mental-health need in the state, and chronic trouble keeping licensed professionals in the field long enough to make a difference. CASSI proposes up to $50,000 in loan forgiveness over ten years, released at 10% per year of full-time qualifying work, covering federal Direct Loans, Direct PLUS Loans, and Perkins Loans.

Eligibility is built around licensure rather than job title, so the program flexes with wherever the work actually happens: Licensed Social Workers and Licensed Independent/Clinical Social Workers, Licensed Professional Counselors, Licensed Chemical Dependency Counselors and Certified Alcohol and Drug Counselors, Certified Nonprofit Professionals and other nonprofit-management credential holders, and Ohio Certified Prevention Specialists and Consultants. A Licensed Chemical Dependency Counselor working crisis intervention still qualifies even though the role isn't strictly an addiction-recovery position — the credential travels with the person, not the job posting. Qualifying employment settings span community mental-health centers, public health departments, substance-abuse treatment centers, nonprofits, schools, Mobile Response and Stabilization Services, and youth and family services organizations.

The funding mechanism is the more interesting design choice: rather than competing for general-fund dollars against every other priority, CASSI is built to draw $30–45 million annually from a tax on recreational marijuana sales, based on revenue Colorado and Illinois have already generated from the same source — treating workforce retention in social services as a direct claim on that revenue rather than an afterthought competing for scraps. State budget allocations, federal workforce-development grants, and philanthropic partnerships fill in around that base. Participants recertify annually with employment and loan documentation, and there's a formal appeals process for denied applications — the administrative backbone that keeps a ten-year forgiveness commitment credible rather than aspirational.

The program is explicitly built to grow: if expanded to cover Master's-level clinicians, the combined forgiveness cap rises to $75,000, and a reserve fund plus adjustable forgiveness rates are built in from the start as contingency against a bad tax-revenue year, rather than leaving that risk for a future legislature to discover.
