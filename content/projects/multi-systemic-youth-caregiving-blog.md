Evidence-based models for coordinating care across child welfare, juvenile justice, mental health, and education exist — Multi-Systemic Therapy among them — and mostly stay out of reach for the families who need them. This piece maps why: services that don't communicate with each other, caseworkers carrying more cases than any one person can track, and mental-health providers with waitlists long enough to make "coordinated care" theoretical.

The disparities aren't evenly distributed. Black youth are roughly 14% of the U.S. population but close to a quarter of children in foster care and 40% of those in juvenile detention — not a coincidence, and not separable from how under-resourced the coordination infrastructure is in the communities that need it most. The piece argues for wraparound services, trauma-informed and culturally responsive practice, and real investment in the workforce, written a year before TRACEs gave the caregiver side of this same problem a name.
