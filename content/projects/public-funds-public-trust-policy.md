Universities, hospitals, and social-service agencies that receive federal funding collect enormous amounts of personal data — and in the absence of a comprehensive federal privacy law, plenty of it gets shared or sold to third parties with little oversight and less consent. The problem isn't a lack of rules so much as a lack of one rule: enforcement is currently scattered across the FTC, HHS, the Department of Education, and others, each with its own jurisdiction and its own gaps, which is functionally the same as no enforcement for anything that falls between them.

The brief proposes a direct lever rather than a new regulatory body: any organization receiving government grants, contracts, or subsidies would be barred from sharing personally identifiable information with third parties for non-essential purposes. Exemptions are narrow and specific — law enforcement, public-health reporting, and IRB-governed academic research — and even those require personal identifiers to be anonymized wherever feasible, so the exemptions can't quietly become the loophole.

Enforcement runs through the funding agencies themselves — Education, HHS, and others — in coordination with the Department of Justice, with funding suspension, monetary penalties, and mandatory remedial measures as the actual teeth behind the requirement. Regular compliance reporting keeps the whole thing auditable rather than self-certifying. It's a narrower ask than a GDPR-style federal privacy law, and that's deliberate: it uses leverage the federal government already has over its own funding recipients, rather than waiting on a comprehensive law that hasn't materialized and may not for years.

The honest obstacles are the ones you'd expect — industry pushback from organizations that rely on data-sharing partnerships for revenue, the coordination burden of getting multiple federal agencies to enforce consistently, and the technical reality that not every funded organization has the infrastructure to anonymize or secure data the way the Act would require. None of that argues against the proposal; it argues for phased enforcement with real investment in agency capacity, which is a fixable problem rather than a fatal one.
