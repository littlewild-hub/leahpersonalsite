Traditional care ethics grounds moral obligation in altruism — we care for the vulnerable because our lives are relationally enmeshed with theirs. This paper argues that framing is vulnerable to exactly the critique care ethics usually draws: unbounded obligation, expected self-sacrifice, no clear limit on what one person owes another. The alternative argued here starts somewhere colder, and I think more defensible.

The argument builds from Martha Nussbaum's account of objectification — instrumentality, denial of autonomy, inertness, fungibility, violability, ownership, denial of subjectivity — and treats perception itself as inherently reductive: to perceive another person at all is to reduce them, within your own experience, to an object you must interpret and relate to in terms of your own needs. That's not a moral failure, it's how consciousness works. Every subject is simultaneously pursuing its own ends (a form of psychological egoism) while also being perceived, and therefore partially objectified, by every other subject it encounters.

The stakes of that objectification are what ground the obligation. Introducing another person into your experience introduces a variable you can't fully control, and their destabilization — through trauma, violation, deprivation — makes them less predictable and more capable of disrupting your own continuity: the employee who quits at a critical moment, the partner who withdraws or retaliates. Care isn't owed because the Other is vulnerable. It's owed because their destabilization is a live threat to the continuity of your own subjective experience, and maintaining their stability is, structurally, how you maintain yours. That reframing does real work: it explains why care obligations scale with proximity and power without requiring either equality (which justice ethics assumes) or a fixed hierarchy of vulnerable-and-capable (which traditional care ethics assumes).

It's a short, dense argument, and the direct predecessor to the "promiration" framework developed afterward, which extends this same logic into a full account of what sustained ethical attention actually requires.
