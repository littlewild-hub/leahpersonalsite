CarbonSmart is a straightforward trade: a $10-per-metric-ton tax on CO2e for industries emitting over 100,000 metric tons a year — coal and gas power plants, steel and automotive and chemical manufacturing, large-scale freight and rail, big agribusiness — projected to generate $100–300 million annually in its early stages. The threshold is deliberately high and the tax deliberately modest at launch, to blunt the standard objection that a carbon tax just pushes business out of state; the proposal is built to be a genuine funding mechanism first and a behavioral lever second, with room to raise the rate roughly $5 per ton every two to five years as it matures.

Revenue splits evenly down the middle into two grant programs. The student side funds Ohio residents in renewable-energy-related majors — engineering, environmental science, sustainable agriculture, energy economics, environmental policy — with awards of $2,500 to $10,000 a year based on need and merit, plus additional post-graduation support for staying in Ohio's clean-energy sector. The business side funds small businesses in solar, wind, energy-efficient building materials, EV infrastructure, waste-to-energy, and sustainable agriculture, with grants of $10,000 to $50,000 usable for product development, scaling operations, or hiring, and priority given to businesses in underserved or high-emission regions specifically — the areas most likely to actually feel a transition away from heavy industry.

Implementation leans on infrastructure that already exists rather than building new bureaucracy from scratch: emissions monitoring runs through the Ohio EPA using the existing federal Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program, oversight sits with a regulatory body appointed under that authority, and university and industry partnerships handle outreach and applicant pipeline rather than a state agency doing it cold. Annual audits track whether the tax is actually reducing emissions and whether grant money is producing the jobs and businesses it's meant to — with the rate and allocation open to adjustment based on what those audits actually show, not fixed in statute and forgotten.
